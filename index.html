How to Play Mafia Party Game: The Complete Guide to Roles, Night Phases, and Moderating Like a Pro

Few party games create the same electric mix of suspicion, laughter, and dramatic betrayal as Mafia. If you have ever wondered how to play Mafia party game nights that your friends talk about for weeks, this guide walks you through everything: the classic mafia game roles, how a werewolf game moderator runs the table, and the strategies that separate memorable games from confusing ones.

What is the Mafia party game?

Mafia — also known as Werewolf in its popular variant — is a social deduction game for 5 to 30+ players. The group is secretly divided into two teams: a small, hidden Mafia that knows each other, and the uninformed Village majority that must find and vote out the Mafia before the Mafia eliminates them. Nobody knows anyone else's secret role at the start, and that information gap is the entire game.

A typical game alternates between two phases. At night, everyone closes their eyes while the moderator wakes each special role in turn: the Mafia silently chooses a victim, the Doctor protects someone, and the Detective investigates a suspect. By day, everyone opens their eyes, learns who (if anyone) died overnight, and debates who among them is secretly Mafia. The day ends with a vote, the accused is eliminated, and the cycle repeats until one side wins.

Mafia game roles explained

Understanding mafia game roles is the single biggest factor in a fair, fun game. Here are the roles used at most tables, from essential to advanced:

Mafia (essential). The informed minority. Each night they silently agree on one player to eliminate. During the day they pretend to be ordinary villagers, deflecting suspicion and steering votes toward innocents. A well-played Mafia member is quiet when accused but loud when accusing others.

Villager (essential). The uninformed majority with no night powers. Villagers win purely through daytime debate, reading body language, and voting. They are the backbone of the game — most players at the table will be villagers.

Doctor (recommended for 7+ players). Each night the Doctor protects one player from the Mafia's attack. A smart Doctor protects quiet, valuable-seeming players — and never reveals themselves too early, because the Mafia will kill a known Doctor immediately.

Detective (recommended for 9+ players). Each night the Detective learns one player's true role. The challenge is sharing that information during the day without painting a target on their own back. Experienced Detectives drop hints and let villagers connect the dots.

Bodyguard (great for 11+ players). Each night the Bodyguard shields one player. If the Mafia attacks the guarded player, the Bodyguard dies instead. It is the game's most dramatic sacrifice, and a well-timed guard can completely flip the momentum.

For larger tables, many groups add their own twists — a Jester who wins by getting voted out, a Mayor whose vote counts double, or a Vigilante villager who can secretly eliminate one player per game. The golden rule for custom roles: every role should create interesting daytime conversation, not just nighttime math.

How many Mafia should there be?

Balance matters more than most beginners realize. Too many Mafia and the village has no chance; too few and the game drags. The classic formula used at experienced tables is floor(total players ÷ 3) Mafia members — so 7 players get 2 Mafia, 9 players get 3, and 12 players get 4. This ratio keeps tension high in both phases without making the Mafia feel hopeless or invincible.

Special roles follow a similar progression. Add a Doctor at 7 players, a Detective at 9, and a Bodyguard at 11. Each addition gives the village one more tool, which keeps the 1:3 ratio fair as the table grows. For very large games (15+), consider two Doctors, or let the Detective investigate twice per night.

The werewolf game moderator: running the night phase

The moderator is the invisible hand of the game. A good werewolf game moderator keeps the night phase silent, orderly, and dramatic — while a sloppy one leaks information that ruins the deduction. Here is the exact sequence most experienced moderators follow:

1. Put the village to sleep. "Everyone close your eyes and lower your heads. No talking, no peeking." Give it a few seconds of genuine silence — this is what makes the night feel real.

2. Wake the Mafia first. "Mafia — open your eyes." The Mafia silently point at their target. The moderator nods to confirm, then: "Mafia — close your eyes." Waking the Mafia first is standard because every other night action is a response to their choice.

3. Wake the Doctor. "Doctor — open your eyes. Point at the player you are protecting tonight." The moderator confirms and sends the Doctor back to sleep.

4. Wake the Detective. "Detective — open your eyes. Point at the player you want to investigate." The moderator silently signals the answer — a thumbs-up for Mafia, thumbs-down for innocent — and puts the Detective back to sleep.

5. Wake the Bodyguard (if in play), then: 6. Wake everyone. "Everyone open your eyes — it is morning." The moderator announces the night's result with theater: if the Doctor saved the target, describe a narrow escape; if someone died, let them give a dramatic farewell speech.

Moderator discipline is everything. Never rush the night, never comment on what you saw, and never let players peek — a single slipped glance can hand the village a free win. If you are moderating for the first time, use a guided prompt tool so you never forget the wake order or what to say.

Running the day phase: debate and voting

The day phase is where Mafia becomes unforgettable. After the night's result is announced, open the floor for discussion. A timed debate — typically 5 minutes for small games, up to 10 for large ones — keeps energy high and prevents one loud player from dominating. When the timer ends, hold the vote: every living player points at their suspect simultaneously, and the player with the most votes is eliminated and reveals their role.

A few day-phase rules keep things fair: dead players may not speak or gesture (no ghost hints); accusations must be defended before the vote; and nobody may show their role card as proof — claims are just words, and Mafia members lie. The game ends when all Mafia are eliminated (village wins) or when the living Mafia equal or outnumber the living villagers (Mafia wins, since the vote can no longer stop them).

Common beginner mistakes

Revealing too early. New Detectives and Doctors announce themselves on day one and are killed that night. Power roles should survive at least two or three nights before claiming.

Voting without reasoning. "I just feel it" votes waste eliminations. Good villagers explain their logic — who was quiet during the night result, who pushed a bad vote yesterday, whose story changed.

Mafia killing obvious targets. New Mafia players always kill the loudest villager. Experienced Mafia kill the quiet observer who is clearly tracking patterns — the loud player is more useful alive as a distraction.

Forgetting the count. Tables lose track of who is alive and how many Mafia might remain. Keep a visible alive/dead tracker — it sharpens every debate and makes the win condition obvious to everyone.

Why pass-and-play dealing beats cards and chits

Traditional Mafia uses playing cards or paper chits for roles, but physical dealing has real problems at parties: someone always peeks, chits get lost under the sofa, and custom roles mean hand-writing twenty slips before the game starts. Pass-and-play dealing on a single phone solves all of it — each player taps to reveal their secret role, memorizes it, and hands the phone on. Nobody sees anyone else's card, setup takes thirty seconds, and the role mix is perfectly balanced every time.

It also removes the most awkward part of hosting: you no longer need to prepare anything. Open the dealer, enter the player count, tweak the roles if you like, and pass the phone. The guided night prompts mean even a first-time moderator runs a flawless night phase, and the alive/dead tracker plus discussion timer handle the bookkeeping that usually falls on whoever is hosting.

Mafia vs. Werewolf: what's the difference?

New players often ask whether Mafia and Werewolf are different games. Mechanically, they are the same social deduction game with different costumes: the Mafia become Werewolves, the village stays the village, and the Detective is often called the Seer. The night/day structure, the voting, and the win conditions are identical. "Werewolf" editions tend to add more exotic roles (the Hunter who takes someone down when killed, the Witch with potions), while classic Mafia stays closer to the Doctor/Detective core. Everything in this guide applies to both — and any good role dealer should handle either theme.

Tips for an unforgettable game night

Dim the lights for the night phase — atmosphere does half the moderator's work. Seat players in a circle so nobody can hide behind anyone. For the first game of the evening, keep it to the core roles so everyone learns the flow; add the Bodyguard and custom roles in game two. And establish one house rule up front: what happens at the table stays at the table — no holding grudges over a well-played betrayal. The best Mafia nights end with the eliminated players laughing the loudest, already plotting their revenge in the next round.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many players do you need to play Mafia?

Mafia works best with 7 to 15 players, but it is playable with as few as 5 and scales to 30 or more. With 5–6 players use 1–2 Mafia and no special roles; add a Doctor at 7 players, a Detective at 9, and a Bodyguard at 11. The classic balance is floor(players ÷ 3) Mafia members.

What is the difference between Mafia and Werewolf?

They are the same social deduction game with different themes. The Mafia become Werewolves, the Detective is often called the Seer, and the night/day structure, voting, and win conditions are identical. Werewolf editions sometimes add extra roles like the Hunter or Witch, but the core gameplay is the same.

Who wins when the Mafia equal the villagers?

The Mafia wins. When the number of living Mafia equals or exceeds the number of living non-Mafia players, the village can no longer vote them out — the Mafia controls the vote. The village wins only when every Mafia member has been eliminated.

Can the Doctor save themselves in Mafia?

In most house rules, yes — the Doctor may protect themselves, but not on consecutive nights. Self-saving every night makes the Doctor nearly unkillable, so the "no consecutive self-save" rule keeps the game balanced. Agree on the rule before the game starts.

What does the Detective do at night?

Each night the Detective secretly investigates one player and learns their true role — the moderator signals it silently, usually with a thumbs-up (Mafia) or thumbs-down (innocent). The Detective's challenge is sharing this information during the day without revealing themselves and becoming the Mafia's next target.

How do you moderate Mafia for the first time?

Follow a fixed script: put everyone to sleep, wake the Mafia first (they choose a target), then the Doctor (chooses a save), then the Detective (investigates one player), then wake everyone and announce the result. Never rush, never comment on what you saw, and keep dead players silent. A guided prompt tool handles the exact wording so you can focus on the drama.
